# Reader profile: evidence-based guidelines

Deep-research synthesis (2026-08-23, three parallel literature reviews: ADHD
reading, autism reading, learning science). Canonical source for the rewrite
prompt. Reader: senior software engineer, AuDHD, on ADHD medication.

## The operating model of the reader

- **Medication restores sustained attention, not working memory.** Stimulant
  meta-analyses (Pievsky & McGrath 2018; Tamminga et al. 2016): sustained
  attention improves (g≈0.22–0.42) but WM gains are smallest and
  non-significant (g≈0.13). Verbal WM deficit in adult ADHD is d≈0.55
  (Alderson et al. 2013). → The text must do the working memory's job.
- **ADHD's signature failure is the *centrality deficit***: under-extraction
  of main points, not details (Miller 2013; Parks 2022 review). Never leave
  the main point to be inferred.
- **Autistic reading accuracy is intact; the cost is rereading.** Eye-tracking
  (Howard et al. 2017): normal comprehension machinery, but less skipping and
  much more second-pass rereading ("cautious reading"). Every ambiguity,
  implicit link, or unmarked irony forces a regression. Refund that budget.
- **Structure closes the gap.** Staged scaffolding eliminated the ADHD–control
  comprehension difference on long expository text (Brann & Sidi 2025);
  deficits concentrate in unstructured tasks (Parks 2022).

## Guidelines (by evidence strength)

### Tier 1 — well-supported

1. **State the central claim of every section explicitly, first; restate at
   the end.** (Centrality deficit; reverse cohesion effect, McNamara.)
2. **Signal aggressively**: informative headings stating the point, numbered
   argument steps ("second of three reasons"), bold first-use of key terms,
   explicit pointer phrases. (Signaling meta-analysis g=0.53, Schneider 2018.)
3. **Resolve every ambiguous referent.** No pronoun/"this" with an antecedent
   more than one sentence back — repeat the noun. (O'Connor & Klein 2004:
   anaphoric cuing was the single best-evidenced text manipulation for autistic
   readers; Open Book cross-over 2019; WM anaphora literature.)
4. **One concept = one term, forever.** No synonym rotation for technical
   referents. (Corollary of 3; also the locked terminology rule.)
5. **Write the connective.** "Because", "therefore", "this contradicts X" —
   convert costly bridging inferences into free reads. (Saldaña & Frith 2007;
   Sansosti 2013; Jolliffe & Baron-Cohen 2000.)
6. **Cut seductive details; keep load-bearing examples.** Interesting-but-
   irrelevant material harms learning (g≈−0.33, Sundararajan & Adesope 2020);
   concrete examples that instantiate a target concept improve it (Rawson
   2015). Test: does removing it break the causal chain or delete an
   instantiation? No → cut.
7. **End-of-chapter retrieval questions with answers.** Testing effect,
   g≈0.50–0.61 across three meta-analyses; feedback amplifies. Cumulative
   questions reaching back to earlier chapters add spacing (Cepeda 2006;
   Uner & Roediger 2018 "both placements" result).
8. **Unpack or drop figurative language; no unmarked irony/hyperbole.** High-
   verbal autistic adults decode it but pay total-time cost; irony/sarcasm/
   exaggeration are their top self-reported barriers (Yaneva 2019).
9. **State authorial positions flatly.** "X. The counterargument is Y. Y fails
   because Z." Social-intent reading is the tax (Brown 2013 meta-analysis:
   autistic readers disproportionately worse on socially-framed text).
10. **Don't dumb down.** Language ability predicts comprehension and this
    reader's is high; vocabulary simplification wastes precision. Simplify
    syntax (no center-embedding, one causal link per sentence), never
    terminology or ideas. Fluent prose + generative interruptions beats
    artificial difficulty (disfluency failed replication).

### Tier 2 — moderate / convergent

11. **2–3 prequestions at chapter start targeting core claims.** Pretesting
    g≈0.54–0.66 but only for targeted content. (Tension: didn't help autistic
    adolescents in O'Connor & Klein — keep them literal and few.)
12. **Advance organizer at chapter start**: conceptual skeleton + link to
    prior chapters, not a heading list. (Small positive effect, Luiten 1980;
    Mayer pretraining; autistic adults request "summary at top", Yaneva 2019.)
13. **Self-explanation prompts at inferential junctures** (2–4/chapter):
    "Pause: why would X imply Y?" with resolution following. (Bisra 2018
    g=0.55; also the staged-prompt mechanism from Brann & Sidi.)
14. **Segment: one idea per short section, micro-summary at boundary.**
    (Rey 2019; time-on-task mind-wandering; length was autistic adults' #1
    complaint.)
15. **Lists for parallel items, tables for comparisons.** (Preference data +
    signaling mechanism; contrastive tables for confusable concepts —
    interleaving works for discrimination, not prose order.)
16. **Key-points recap at end as the answer key**, not a substitute for
    retrieval questions. (Summaries are re-presentation, not retrieval.)

### Tier 3 — house conventions (mechanism-plausible, unvalidated numbers)

17. Sentences ~≤25 words when carrying causal chains; never center-embedded.
18. Short paragraphs, generous whitespace.
19. Predictable chapter skeleton book-wide (reader always knows what's next).
20. De-hedge: replace "it could perhaps be argued" with calibrated claims and
    named exceptions. When uncertainty is real, quantify it.
21. Novelty inside the skeleton: open sections with the surprising fact or
    concrete case; never with throat-clearing. (Interest reduces
    mind-wandering; novelty aids ADHD attention — child data.)

### Anti-rules (evidence says don't)

- No decorative images or entertaining tangents (seductive details).
- No perceptual difficulty tricks.
- No relying on the reader to summarize/reread (low utility).
- Retention apparatus scales down for short chapters (<~1,000 words) and off
  for non-prose (reference/appendix) material.
